Ask evaluation: is the answer right, not only well sourced?

The Scripture harness already guarantees where an answer's evidence comes from: every verse it cites carries a canonical id and a content commitment that is checked before the answer leaves. That settles authenticity. It does not settle the question a reader actually cares about: did the agent understand the question and use that authentic Scripture correctly to answer it? This page describes the evaluation built for that question: the question and answer sets, the record that sits behind every case, the judge and the verdict it produces, how the results reach the Expert Workbench, the loop used to improve the agent, and the first baseline. It also sets the approach beside the common alternative, grading against a rubric alone.

The problem this solves

A Scripture answer can fail in three different places, and each needs a different kind of check.

Failure Example What catches it
The source is wrong A misquoted verse, or a verse that does not exist. Structural. Canonical ids and signed content commitments; the harness strikes a quotation it cannot verify. Not part of this evaluation.
The source is real but misused A genuine verse cited for a claim it does not support, read out of its context. This evaluation: the scriptural-support dimension.
The answer is wrong or incomplete The question is answered with true sentences that miss the point, contradict what the passage teaches, present one tradition's reading as the only Christian one, or claim more certainty than the text allows. This evaluation: intent, essential claims, theological scope and restraint.

Quotation-accuracy harnesses, which compare a model's quotation with the text a Bible API returns, measure only the first row. The Scripture agent does not need one: its citations are verified by construction (every baseline run read citations-verified 1.00). The work is in the second and third rows, and there a judgment about meaning is unavoidable. The design question is how to make that judgment grounded and auditable rather than a model's opinion.

The whole path, in one picture

Reference answers and rubrics: what each supplies

There are two broad ways to grade an open answer. A reference answer (a "gold" answer) is a trusted example of what a correct answer says. A rubric is a set of explicit criteria a grader applies: what must be present, what counts as an error, how much variation is acceptable. They are not alternatives so much as two halves of one job, and the way they are combined decides how much a score can be trusted.

Approach What it gives Where it falls short
A reference answer alone A concrete standard: the grader can see what a correct answer says. One preferred paragraph. A correct answer worded differently, or one that makes the same points in another order, looks like a miss unless the grader is told what actually matters.
A generic rubric ("accuracy, 1 to 5") Reusable across every question; cheap to write; covers qualities no single answer shows (tone, pastoral care, honesty about disagreement). The grader decides what "accurate" means for each question from its own knowledge, so the score inherits the grader's views and cannot be audited case by case. Two graders can disagree with no way to say which is right.
A question-specific rubric Names, for this question, what must be included and what would be wrong. Captures much of a reference answer's value without requiring one preferred wording. Someone has to write it for every question, and its standard is only as defensible as its author; without a published source, "what must be included" is an opinion written down.
This harness: a reference-anchored, question-specific record, judged on reusable dimensions The essential claims and their passages come from human-written, published answers, not from the grader or the evaluator; the five dimensions are reusable across every set; each verdict says which claim was missed and why, so a person can check it. The reference carries its source's perspective, which must be said on every case and kept apart from "wrong" (see the difference verdict). Intent, acceptable alternatives and material errors still need a reviewer.

In short: the dimensions are the reusable rubric, the record is the question-specific rubric, and the reference answer is what makes the record defensible. A judge that checks claims rather than wording permits different valid answers while still testing correctness. Gold answers do not remove semantic judgment; they make it grounded and auditable.

Questions worth asking of any rubric-based evaluation. Is the rubric generic or question-specific? Does each question name its expected claims, or carry a reference answer, or neither? Who performs the grading (people, a model, or both), and who validates the grader against people? A sample of rubrics with graded responses answers all four at once.

The evaluation record behind every case

Every question in the catechism and study-note sets carries a record. It is built by code from the source's own structure wherever the source provides it, and left empty for a reviewer where it does not, never filled in by a model.

Field What it holds Where it comes from
ask The question, as a person would type it. The catechism's own question; for a verse note, a proposed question about the note's word in its verse (below).
intent What the person is actually asking. Reviewer; until then the question is read plainly.
reference_answer The published answer, verbatim. The source.
essentialClaims[] The points a good answer conveys, each with supportedBy: the passages the source itself gives for that point, and why. Code: a catechism's proof-text markers split its answer into clauses with their passages; a note's sentences carry the passages the note cites.
acceptableAlternatives[] Other formulations or readings that also satisfy a claim. Reviewer.
materialErrors[] Statements that fail an answer outright. Reviewer.
perspective Whose teaching the reference states: a particular confession's, or shared Christian teaching. Code, from the source; said on every case.
split Development, or held out. A hash of the case id: one in five held out, for ever.

The record states "what this source answers", never "what Christianity teaches". That one rule is what lets the harness hold the agent to several traditions' standards at once without adopting any of them.

The question and answer sets

Two tracks. The published-literature track is the gold: answers written by people, adopted after checking the edition and its licence. The benchmark track projects open evaluation sets as their authors published them, with their own scoring rules. Nothing in either track is written by a model, and none of it enters a training set or a search index the agent reads.

Every set states its tradition on every case. A low score on a confession's distinctive points is first a difference of standard and only then, if the reviewer agrees, an error.

How the questions are made

A catechism already asks its questions. A verse note does not: it is a comment headed by a word or phrase of the verse. The question for a note is about that word, in that verse, and nothing more. "What does Paul mean when he says believers are justified in Romans 3:24?" is the shape. A model (Claude Haiku 5.5) proposes the wording; code decides whether it may be used:

  • one question, at most 30 words, naming the verse;
  • asking about the note's heading phrase, by its own words;
  • quoting no six-word run of the note (curly and straight apostrophes normalised; a stored question is re-checked whenever the rule changes);
  • never naming the note or its source.

Of 10,949 notes, 9,875 have an admissible question; 1,074 were dropped by those rules (the most common reasons: the heading phrase was not asked about, the note was quoted, the verse was not named). A second check asks whether the note actually answers the question; a question the note does not answer is the set's fault and is taken out of the agent's denominator. A notes question can still lead, handing the agent part of the answer in the asking. That is the reviewer's first job, and the reason coverage on these sets is read with care.

The judge and the verdict

One call per answer. The judge (Claude Haiku 5.5, named on every number) is blind to which system wrote the answer, sees the record, the passages the answer cited, and the answer, and decides five things. Code then turns those into one verdict by thresholds fixed before any result was read, and pinned by a test.

Dimension The judge decides In the verdict?
Intent Did the answer address what was asked? addressed · partly · no Yes: must be addressed
Essential claims For each claim: stated (same substance, any words, or an acceptable alternative) · differs · contradicted · absent Yes: no contradiction, and at least two thirds of the decidable claims stated
Scriptural support For each passage the answer cites: does it, read in context, support the claim it is attached to? Not whether it matches the reference's passages. Yes: at least 0.8 where the answer cites anything
Theological scope Where the reference is one tradition's teaching, does the answer distinguish shared Christian teaching from that tradition's reading? Diagnostic, until calibrated
Restraint Doctrinal or factual assertions with nothing behind them; certainty beyond the text; a material error from the record Material errors yes; the rest diagnostic, until calibrated

The verdict is good (all of it holds), good with a difference (all of it holds, and the answer takes another position on at least one disputed reading) or bad (anything else, with the reasons on the verdict).

Why "differs" exists. The first run scored two answers as contradicting their reference where both were sound readings of passages Christians genuinely read in more than one way (whether a verse describes two stages of Christ's work or two natures; whether a word names a church office or service in general). A judge that cannot tell "wrong" from "another recognised reading" measures how closely the agent echoes one tradition. Now a claim can be differs, with the competing reading named; it is counted and shown, never failed. Contradicted is kept for what the text plainly says or Christians broadly share.

Code graders run beside the judge on every answer: whether the agent refused (including its own "no sourced passages" decline), whether it cited, whether every citation's commitment verified, how many of the gold's verses it cited, and whether it reproduced any eight-word run of a private gold answer (which would mean it retrieved the gold instead of answering). The open benchmark sets keep their own judges and aggregation rules (banded scores, behaviours and failure modes, triage strata) and are never pooled with the record verdict.

Licensed text stays private

The study notes are another publisher's text, used by permission and not for redistribution. So the set that carries a note lives in a private evaluation store outside every repository; the repository and this reader's bundle hold a stub with only the question, the verse and its strata. The judge's reasoning, which quotes the note, stays in the private store; a committed result keeps verdict labels and numbers only. Every committed file is scanned for any eight-word run of any note (541,000 runs checked); what the scan finds is Scripture both quote, and one note heading it found has been removed from the stubs.

The Expert Workbench

The Christian Ask sets are a domain of the existing Expert Workbench, not a separate tool. Each run writes the harness's standard experiment record (arms, graders with means and pass rates, paired lifts, every run's verdicts with the judge that made them), so the Workbench shows a scorecard by set, the questions the agent struggles with, and each answer's verdicts, with no change to the app. Results from different judges, or different sets, are never pooled.

The improvement loop

  1. Freeze the questions. The suite is 645 question ids across every set (five development notes from each of the 66 books, 30 from each of four catechisms, the benchmark samples), recorded once so every later run asks exactly the same questions whatever happens to the set files.
  2. Run the baseline. Each question is a live ask of the Scripture agent in a fresh conversation; the answers are stored, then judged.
  3. Change the agent. A prompt, a skill, retrieval, the model.
  4. Run the same questions again and compare, paired per question: the good rate and each dimension before and after, a bootstrap 95 % interval on each difference, an exact McNemar test on the flips, the list of answers that went good to bad and bad to good with their reasons, and the essential claims the weakest answers missed. The comparison refuses runs judged by different judges or on different samples.
  5. Confirm on the held-out fifth before keeping a change. Development cases are tuned against; held-out cases are only looked at.

At about 100 paired questions, a change of roughly ten points in good rate is the smallest the interval can separate from noise; the 329 notes questions see a good deal less. A full run costs the agent's 645 asks plus a few dollars of judging; iterating on the notes sets alone halves it.

What it says about the agent.

  • It answers the question and cites sound passages (intent 0.95, support 0.98, no citation failed verification), and contradicted a reference on no point outside a disputed reading.
  • Its weakness is completeness. Every bad notes answer failed on claims: the agent answers well in general and leaves out the particular points the reference makes. Notes with four or more claims read 39 % good against 84 % for single-claim notes.
  • It is weakest on the Epistles, the histories and the wisdom books (52–54 % good) and strongest on the Law and the Gospels (72–76 %).
  • It declines under a burst. The first pass read 35 % good: under about a dozen concurrent asks, the agent answered "I don't have sourced passages for that question yet." instantly to 244 questions that it answers fully when asked alone. The harness now retries a decline after a pause and counts a persisting one as a refusal; the behaviour itself is a finding for the agent's operators.

How far to trust the verdict

Check Result What it shows
Repeatability: the same 60 answers judged twice good vs bad agree 98 % (κ 0.96); three labels 93 % (κ 0.87) The judge is consistent with itself. Not that it is right.
A cheaper judge from another provider, on the same 60 κ 0.53 against the named judge; it missed 9 of the 16 bad answers Rejected. A judge that calls bad answers good hides the failures.
Agreement with a person Not yet measured The one number that turns a reading into a measurement. Reviewer sheets are written (stratified toward bad and differing answers); the report gives a confusion matrix and κ, and the verdict is not quoted until κ ≥ 0.6 on good vs bad. Below that the judge's prompt changes, never the thresholds.

What comes next

  • A person reviews the judge. Fill the reviewer sheets (verdict, and whether each question is fair, leading or off-point) and read the agreement.
  • Reviewers complete the records where the source cannot: intent, acceptable alternatives, material errors, and which claims are disputed readings.
  • Repeats. Three answers per question, paired, before a change is called real.
  • Improve the agent against completeness, the weakness the baseline names, and confirm on the held-out fifth.
