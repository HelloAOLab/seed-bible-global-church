Ask evaluation: is the answer right, not only well sourced?

The Scripture harness already guarantees where an answer's evidence comes from: every verse it cites carries a canonical id and a content commitment that is checked before the answer leaves. That settles authenticity. It does not settle the question a reader actually cares about: did the agent understand the question and use that authentic Scripture correctly to answer it? This page describes the evaluation built for that question: the question and answer sets, the record that sits behind every case, the judge and the verdict it produces, how the results reach the Expert Workbench, the loop used to improve the agent, and the first baseline. It also sets the approach beside the common alternative, grading against a rubric alone.

Status: in development; every score on this page is provisional. An outside review (29 October 2026) found the design sound and its first scores premature: the first scoring confused covering a reference answer, answering the question and being correct. This page describes the revised evaluator, what the review changed, and what is still needed before a score is used to steer the agent. The most valuable next result is evidence that the evaluator tells apart a concise correct answer, a genuinely incomplete one, a justified alternative and a well-cited wrong answer.

The problem this solves

A Scripture answer can fail in three different places, and each needs a different kind of check.

Failure Example What catches it
The source is wrong A misquoted verse, or a verse that does not exist. Structural: canonical ids and signed content commitments; the harness strikes a quotation it cannot verify. Not part of this evaluation.
The source is real but misused A genuine verse cited for a claim it does not support, read out of its context. This evaluation: citation support.
The answer is wrong, incomplete or overreaching True sentences that miss the point; a claim that contradicts the text; one tradition's reading presented as the only Christian one; an extra assertion with nothing behind it. This evaluation: intent, required claims, additional claims, scope.

Quotation-accuracy harnesses, which compare a quotation with the text a Bible API returns, measure only the first row; the Scripture agent's citations are verified by construction. The work is in the other two, where a judgment about meaning is unavoidable. The design question is how to make that judgment grounded, auditable and validated.

The whole path, in one picture

Reference answers and rubrics: what each supplies

A reference answer is a trusted example of what a correct answer says. A rubric is a set of explicit criteria: what must be present, what counts as an error, how much variation is acceptable. Rubric-based grading can be used with or without reference answers, and a rubric can be generic or written for each question.

Approach What it gives Where it falls short
A reference answer alone A concrete standard the grader can see. One preferred paragraph; it usually says more than a question needs, and a correct answer worded differently looks like a miss unless the grader is told what matters.
A generic rubric ("accuracy, 1 to 5") Reusable; covers qualities no single answer shows (tone, pastoral care, honesty about disagreement). The grader decides what "accurate" means for each question from its own knowledge, so the score inherits its views and is hard to audit case by case.
A question-specific rubric Names, for this question, what must be included and what would be wrong; permits different valid answers. Someone has to write and review it for every question. A carefully reviewed one can be stronger than a reference record extracted automatically.
This harness A question-specific record whose candidate claims and passages come from human-written published answers, judged on reusable dimensions, each verdict naming the claim that decided it. Publication improves traceability; it does not make a statement necessary for a given question or correct. Which claims a question requires is a reviewer's decision; until it is made the record is a proposal.

So the published answer is evidence for the rubric, not the rubric itself: the dimensions are the reusable criteria, the record is the question-specific rubric, and the reference is where its candidate requirements come from. A judge that checks claims rather than wording permits different valid answers while still testing correctness.

Questions worth asking of any rubric-based evaluation. Is the rubric generic or question-specific? Does each question name expected claims, carry a reference answer, or neither? Who grades (people, a model, or both), and who validates the grader against people? A sample of rubrics with graded responses answers all four.

The evaluation record behind every case

Every case in the catechism and study-note sets carries a record. Code builds what the source supplies; a model may propose what it does not; a reviewer decides. A proposal is marked as one on every case and never overwrites a reviewer.

Field What it holds Where it comes from
ask The question, as a person would type it. The catechism's own question; for a verse note, a proposed question about the note's word in its verse.
task What the question asks the answer to do: explain_text · explain_doctrine · describe_tradition · compare_readings · assess_evidence · fact. Proposed; reviewed.
depth How much the question asks for: brief · standard · detailed. A brief verse explanation is not held to a doctrinal survey. Proposed; reviewed.
reference_answer The published answer, verbatim. The source.
claims[] The reference's candidate claims, each with the passages the source gives for it. Code: proof-text markers split a catechism answer; a note's sentences carry the passages it cites.
roles[] For each claim: required (needed to answer this question correctly; missing it can fail the answer) · supporting (makes the answer substantially better; a quality score) · optional (elaboration or application; never penalised). Proposed; a reviewer decides.
acceptableAlternatives[] Other readings that satisfy a claim, each with its own sources and a reviewer's approval. Reviewer.
materialErrors[] Statements that fail an answer outright. Reviewer.
perspective Whose teaching the reference states. Code, from the source; said on every case.
group, split Which related cases it belongs with, and whether it is development, validation or test. Code (below).

On the 541 record cases of the current suite, the proposals mark 653 claims required, 508 supporting and 251 optional: usually one or two required claims a question, and none for 12 questions the reference does not actually answer.

How the questions are made

A verse note is headed by a word or phrase of its verse; its question is about that word, in that verse, and nothing more ("What does Paul mean when he says believers are justified in Romans 3:24?" is the shape). A model proposes the wording; code decides whether it may be used: one question of at most 30 words, naming the verse, asking about the heading phrase, quoting no six-word run of the note (apostrophes normalised; every stored question is re-checked when a rule changes), never naming the note. 9,875 of 10,949 notes have an admissible question. A second check asks whether the note actually answers the question; a question it does not answer is an invalid case, decided before any answer is read, excluded from both sides of any comparison, and counted with its reason. A question can still lead; that is the reviewer's first check.

The judge and its outcomes

One call per answer by a named judge (Claude Haiku 5.5), blind to which system wrote the answer. It sees the record with its roles, the text of every passage the answer cites, and the answer, which is delimited and declared to be data: an answer that tries to instruct the judge cannot, and cannot close its own delimiter. It returns:

Measure What it asks Effect
Intent Did the answer do the task the question asks? Must be addressed.
Required claims Each stated (any words, or an approved alternative), differs, contradicted or absent. All must be stated; one that differs sends the answer to adjudication.
Supporting claims The same, for claims that make the answer better. A quality score; a contradiction fails.
Citation support Does each cited passage, read with its words, support the claim it is attached to? Cannot-tell where a verse alone is not enough context. At least 0.8 where the answer cites; no citation at all fails a task that needs evidence.
Additional claims Every substantive claim the answer makes beyond the reference: supported, defensible, unsupported or wrong, and major or minor. A wrong claim, or a major unsupported one, fails an otherwise complete answer; a defensible insight the reference lacks is never penalised.
Scope Where the reference is one tradition's, does the answer distinguish shared teaching from that reading? Diagnostic until calibrated.

Differences depend on the task. Allowing another reading is right; making it automatically acceptable is not. When the question asks what a named tradition teaches, a different tradition's position is wrong for that question. When it asks what a verse means, a recognised alternative reading is differs, and differing on a required claim sends the answer to a reviewer rather than letting the judge decide that the alternative is established. Recognising several readings does not make them equally supported, and a well-supported conclusion favouring one is not penalised.

Outcome Meaning Counted as
good Every required claim stated; nothing required or supporting contradicted; no wrong or major unsupported addition; citations support their claims; intent addressed. good
bad Anything else; the reasons are on the verdict. bad
needs-adjudication It would be good, but a required claim differs. neither; listed for a reviewer
judge-undecided The judge said it could not judge, or no claim was decidable. neither; counted
invalid-case The question or record is defective. excluded from both sides, with its reason
execution failure The ask or the judge call failed. neither; counted apart from abstention

The thresholds were fixed before any result was read and are pinned by tests covering the review's edge cases: a concise correct answer, a genuinely incomplete one, a justified alternative, a well-cited wrong answer, every claim differing, no decidable claim, no citations where evidence is needed, one citation on several claims, a major and a minor unsupported addition, and an answer attempting to instruct the judge.

What the agent could see when it answered

The review found an apparent conflict: the study notes are among the writings the Scripture agent holds, yet they are also gold. Both are true, and what matters is the route. The suite measures the agent's default ask, which searches the signed Bible and reads verses from it; it does not read the commentary index or the per-verse dossiers, and every citation it returned in the baseline was a verse of the signed edition. By retrieval, that run is a reference withheld experiment. The verse ask behind the reader's study companion does read a verse dossier that can include the study note on that verse; a run of that route is a production retrieval experiment, where using a relevant commentary is legitimate, and must be labelled as such. Neither route can rule out what the answering model learned in pretraining.

Each run records its route, edition and agent model in a contract (below). Still to add, with the agent's operators: the effective retrieval filters and the evidence actually supplied to the answering model, per answer. The text-overlap check (no answer shares an eight-word run with its gold) stays as a diagnostic: it can show copying; it cannot show access.

Splits that stay independent

A stable hash of each case id is reproducible but not independent: a catechism question, its explanation's sub-questions and its yes/no exposition teach the same thing, and nearby notes share an argument. So related cases are grouped first (a catechism question with its explanations and its yes/no sub-questions; the notes on one chapter; a benchmark perturbation with its scenario) and whole groups are assigned: development 70 % (tuned against), validation 15 % (decides whether a change is kept), test 15 % (reserved for occasional release decisions, never for choosing between changes). No group spans two splits. The frozen suite now holds only development cases: 477 of the original 645, the rest having moved to validation or test.

Licensed text stays private

The study notes are another publisher's text, used by permission and not for redistribution. The set that carries a note lives in a private evaluation store; the repository and this reader's bundle hold a stub with the question, the verse and its strata. The judge's reasoning, which quotes the note, stays private; a committed result keeps labels and numbers. Every committed file is scanned for eight-word runs of any note; what the scan finds is Scripture both quote.

The Expert Workbench

The Christian Ask sets are a domain of the existing Expert Workbench. Each run writes the harness's standard experiment record, so the Workbench shows a scorecard by set, the questions the agent struggles with and each answer's verdicts. Screens for the work the review assigns to people (reviewing a record's roles, adjudicating a difference, two-reviewer calibration) are the next piece of the Workbench; today that work is done in reviewer sheets.

The improvement loop

  1. Freeze the questions (477 development ids) and the contract: the judge model and a hash of its prompt, the verdict version and thresholds, a digest of every case as judged, the route, edition and agent model, and the concurrency. A comparison refuses two runs whose contracts differ, and leaves out any case whose record changed between them.
  2. Run the baseline: a live ask per question in a fresh conversation, answers stored, then judged.
  3. Change the agent.
  4. Run the same questions again and compare, paired per question: the primary metric, declared before any comparison (the good rate on the record sets), then the dimensions, each with a 95 % interval from a cluster bootstrap that resamples whole groups and repeats together, an exact McNemar test on the flips, and the cases that flipped each way with their reasons.
  5. Diagnose before fixing. For each missed required claim, the comparison says whether the answer cited the claim's own passage (the point was dropped in composition) or not (retrieval or selection). The full attribution (absent from the corpus, not retrieved, retrieved but not selected, selected but not written, removed by a check, or mis-graded) needs the agent's per-step evidence record joined to the claim, and is the next diagnostic.
  6. Keep a change only if it holds on validation; look at test rarely.

Reliability is measured, not hidden: the first answer a user would have received is kept beside any retried one, a decline that a retry later recovers still counts against availability, and latency includes retries. How small a change the comparison can see depends on how many answers flip and in which direction, not on a fixed sample size; the interval is the test.

What the re-judging shows about the evaluator. Under the earlier rule, notes with four or more claims passed 39 % and single-claim notes 84 %. Most of that gap was the record demanding more than the question asked, as the review suspected, not the agent answering complex questions worse.

How far to trust the verdict

Check Result What it shows
Repeatability: 60 answers judged twice good vs bad 98 % (κ 0.96) The judge is consistent with itself, not that it is right.
A cheaper judge from another provider, same 60 κ 0.53 with the named judge; 9 of the named judge's 16 bad answers called good The two judges disagree. Which is right is unknown until people adjudicate; the cheaper judge was not adopted.
Agreement with people Sheets ready, not yet reviewed The test that turns a reading into a measurement (below).

The calibration. 130 answers, stratified across traditions, tasks, claim counts and verdicts, are on two identical sheets for two qualified reviewers working independently. Each reviewer first judges the record (is the question fair, are the required claims really required) and only then the answer, so an unfair record is not inherited; the judge's verdict is not on the sheet. Disagreements are adjudicated. The report gives reviewer-to-reviewer agreement (the ceiling), and against the adjudicated verdict: false passes (the judge passes what people consider materially wrong; the risk that matters most), false fails, whether the judge names the right reason, and all of it by tradition and task. A third of the sheet is held back: the judge's prompt is changed against the rest and the held-back part is read once, at the end. A single κ threshold is not the test; rare false passes on material errors, and agreement close to the reviewers' own, are. Known judge biases (preferring longer answers, answer position, its own model family) are tested for, not assumed away.
