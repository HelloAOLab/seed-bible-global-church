ask-writings v3: read the library once, answer by argument

Specification, for review. Revision 3, 1 November 2026. Status: proposed — deliberately bold. Revision 2 folded an outside review into the existing pipeline. This revision changes the pipeline itself. Today every question starts from zero: fifteen search channels, a pool of ~500 passages, and a model that reads them and reconstructs who teaches what and why. The proposal: read the whole library once, offline, into a graph of attributed propositions typed by our ontology and anchored to the original-language words of Scripture. Then answer a question by finding paths through that graph and composing an argument in which every step is a record. The model writes prose over a verified argument; it no longer has to discover the argument inside a heap of passages.

1. Why change the shape

What we measured (dev slice, blind Haiku judge) Ours Apologist Bonfire
Covers the reference answer's claims 0.36 0.62 0.54
Addresses the question 0.67 0.86 0.82
Scripture support 0.93 0.84 0.54
Strict good answer 0.19 0.33 0.31
  • Our answers are the best supported and the least complete. Evidence quality is not the gap. The composer receives fragments and has to rebuild the teaching from them. Switching the model to gpt-5-mini did not close the gap.
  • The ontology is nearly silent at answer time. The knowledge-base, concept and doctrine channels supplied about 3% of cited evidence. Ask cannot reach any of these, or reaches them only as text to be re-read: 162 doctrine propositions, 231 commitments, 65 argument chains with defeat conditions, 2,861 code-admitted interpretation claims, 17,639 cross-references and the story layer.
  • The original-language writings are present but not used as language. Beside the English, the index holds 25,397 original-language texts (Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek, Latin, Syriac, Coptic, Ge'ez…). In the r1 runs, 2,141 of 22,135 gathered writing passages (about 10%) were in an original language. They were retrieved by meaning and then read as opaque text. Nothing joins them to Scripture's own words: no alignment links a Father's Greek or a midrash's Hebrew to the lemma of the verse it discusses. Our Scripture tokens carry Strong's numbers and morphology, and that join key sits idle.
  • We index the writings as documents, but they are writings about Scripture. A homily on John 3 is a set of claims about John 3, made by a speaker in a tradition at a date. A question needs that structure, and passage chunks erase it.

2. The five ideas

  1. The proposition is the unit (§3). Index self-contained, attributed statements. Dense X Retrieval showed that propositions beat passages as retrieval units, most of all under a fixed token budget, which is exactly the composer's constraint. Ours also carry the speaker, stance, tradition, date and exact span.
  2. The original-language word is the join key (§4). Every proposition about Scripture is anchored to the verse and the lemmas it concerns. A Greek homily, a Latin commentary, a Hebrew midrash and an English study note on ἀγάπη in 1 John 4:8 then meet at the same node, whatever language each was written in.
  3. Read the library once (§5). Extraction runs offline on our own GPU, a fixed cost, instead of per question on Haiku, the metered cost. The work moves from every answer into one build.
  4. Answer by path search, then argument (§6). Retrieval walks the graph from the question's anchors, with no model calls online (as in PropRAG). It returns chains, such as verse → lemma → concept → proposition → who holds it → the counter-position. The composer receives an argument map instead of a pool.
  5. Perspectives are lenses over one graph (§6.3). A tradition is a named profile of commitments. "What does the Heidelberg teach?", "what do Catholics hold?" and "how have Christians read this?" read the same graph through different lenses. There is never a hidden default.

3.2 Edges that carry the reasoning

  • about: proposition → verse / lemma / entity / concept (what it is about, at the finest anchor available).
  • supports / attacks / qualifies: proposition → proposition (argument structure, typed by scheme).
  • sameAs / paraphrases: between propositions from different sources. This clustering lets an answer say "Augustine, Calvin and the Westminster divines all hold…" from one node.
  • quotes / alludes / parallels / fulfils: verse → verse (the 17,639 OpenBible pairs, TSK and the scroll quotations).
  • dependsOn: source → source (Matthew Henry read Calvin; a catechism cites its proof text). Agreement among dependent sources is then not counted as independent witness.
  • defeatedBy: proposition → defeat condition (a disputed date, a textual variant, a contested lexical sense).

3.3 Storage

GraphDB holds the graph as the authority, in named graphs per source family and licence, as today. The runtime reads a projection in D1 and Vectorize:

  • one row per proposition (text, anchors, speaker, stance, date, span id, review status);
  • an adjacency table for the reasoning edges;
  • a vector per proposition.

GraphDB Free must not be queried per question, because it runs only two queries at a time, so D1 is the online store. The projection gets its own database, clear of the 10 GB writings database.

4. The original-language spine

No other Ask app can do this part. None of them has, in one store, the frozen Hebrew and Greek with morphology, the Septuagint, the scrolls and 25,000 original-language writings.

  1. Lemma anchoring. When a writing quotes or discusses a verse, align its words to that verse's tokens. Use the publisher's citation, quotation matching (the method that found 436 scroll quotations at 0.989 precision), or cross-lingual alignment for Greek and Hebrew writings. The proposition then points at lemmas, not only at a verse.
  2. Language-independent retrieval. A question about "the Word" in John 1 resolves to λόγος (G3056). The graph returns Philo's λόγος propositions, the Targums' מימרא, the Fathers in Greek and in Latin (verbum) and the English commentaries. They come back because they share the lemma node or an aligned concept, not because their English happened to embed near the question.
  3. Usage as evidence. Today lemma_occurrences serves word studies only (16 of 43 r1 answers touched it). Promote it: a sense claim such as "ἀγάπη here means…" is supported by the lemma's distribution across Scripture, the Septuagint and the period writings, computed by code.
  4. The composer still writes English. An original-language span reaches the composer with its aligned English, the lemma gloss and the anchor. It never arrives as opaque Greek the composer must translate on the fly.
  5. No English Bible wording enters the graph (the programme rule). Alignment is to the source tokens; the English is the RBS or the edition the reader chose.

5. Reading the library (offline)

5.1 The extraction pipeline

  1. Segment each unit into sentences, keeping the unit id and offsets.
  2. Propositionise: split the sentences into self-contained propositions and decontextualise them, resolving "he", "this doctrine" and "the apostle" from the surrounding section. Dense X trained a small "propositionizer" on a large model's outputs, and we do the same: Haiku labels a few thousand units and our model on RunPod does the rest.
  3. Attribute: record who asserts the proposition, with what stance, in which tradition and at which date. The date comes from the chronology and is never invented. Stance is where the classic trap lies: a commentator reporting a view he rejects.
  4. Anchor: link verses (citations parsed by code), lemmas (§4), and entities and concepts (resolved to IRIs by code against the KB).
  5. Relate: the model proposes candidate supports, attacks and paraphrase edges between propositions on the same anchor. Each is scored, and those above a threshold are admitted with their score kept.
  6. Admit by code, on five checks:
    • every span resolves, and the proposition's content words appear in it or in its alignment;
    • every anchor is a real IRI;
    • no date appears that the source lacks;
    • no six-word run of a held English Bible appears;
    • the licence permits storing the span.

5.2 What to read first

Wave Material Why
0 What we already hold as structure: doctrine, interpretation claims, story layer, chronology, cross-references projection only; no extraction
1 Confessions and catechisms (public-domain editions), the Fathers' commentaries on Scripture, the period writings (Philo, Josephus, 1 Enoch, Qumran sectarian texts) densest in claims about Scripture; covers most question families
2 Rabbinic commentary and Targums (Sefaria), medieval and Reformation commentaries reception history, and lemma alignment in Hebrew
3 The long tail of the 3.87M units, chosen by measured retrieval demand read what questions actually reach

Quarantine is built in, not bolted on. A source that is a reference answer in an evaluation set (the Reformation Study Bible notes, the catechism gold) is extracted into its own named graph, and the independent-synthesis track never loads it.

5.3 Search aids (never evidence)

  • Question forms per proposition (doc2query): the two or three questions it answers.
  • Context headers per unit before embedding (contextual retrieval): author, work, section, the verse discussed, stance.
  • Community summaries per verse and per concept (as in GraphRAG): a short map of the positions on it, rebuilt when its propositions change. This lets "how have Christians read Romans 9?" start from an overview instead of 200 propositions.

6. Answering: paths, lenses, arguments

6.1 Anchor

One small call (or our plan model) turns the question into a frame: its family; its anchors (verses, lemmas, entities, concepts), resolved to IRIs by code; its lens (§6.3); and its answer form. Today's classify prompt is 16k tokens, mostly a works catalog. The frame needs only the question and a short list of capabilities.

6.2 Path search (no model calls)

  • Seed the graph with the anchors and with the top propositions by vector and lexical match, question forms included.
  • Spread relevance outward from the seeds (personalised PageRank, as in HippoRAG 2). Then run a beam search over proposition paths (as in PropRAG): extend each path along about, supports, attacks and paraphrases edges, score it on relevance to the question and on the coherence of the chain, and keep the best k.
  • Return chains. For example: John 1:1 → λόγος → "the Word is a divine person distinct from the Father" (held: Athanasius, the Nicene Creed; attacked by: Arius, as reported in Athanasius) → defeat condition: "θεός without the article" → a Greek grammar proposition.
  • Apply a diversity constraint: at most n chains through one source family, so one prolific author cannot fill the answer.

6.3 Lenses

The question says Lens The answer
a named source ("the Heidelberg", "Augustine") that source's assertions explains that source faithfully, as theirs
a named tradition ("Catholics", "the Reformers") that tradition's profile explains that tradition
a perspective the reader chose the reader's profile answers within it and says so
nothing, and the question is settled across traditions consensus: propositions held across at least 3 independent profiles answers directly; "Christians have consistently held…" is now a computed statement
nothing, and the question is disputed all profiles with assertions a direct answer naming the positions and what each rests on

The graph decides whether a question is settled or disputed: do independent profiles assert conflicting propositions on these anchors? The model's sense of it does not decide. This replaces the current "reading most Christians hold" rule.

6.4 Argument, then prose

  1. Code assembles an argument map from the chains: the direct answer; its supports, each a proposition with its span and the verse it rests on; the qualifications; and the alternatives, if the lens calls for them.
  2. The composer (Haiku 5.5) writes prose over the map in one call, in sections, with every sentence tagged with the map node it renders. It may leave nodes out. It may not add a claim that has no node, except a labelled synthesis (§8).
  3. The answer streams section by section, as each section passes its checks.

6.5 When the graph is thin

Coverage is computed: does the map have a direct answer, at least one Scripture support, and the positions the lens requires? If not, the harness falls back to today's passage retrieval for the missing slot only, and the trace says so. The graph should never make an answer worse than today's pipeline; it should make the common case better.

7. Plans as competency questions

Ontology engineering already has the idea we need: competency questions, the questions an ontology must be able to answer, each with its query. Our question families become competency-question templates over the graph:

Family Template (graph operations)
What does X teach about Y assertions by X → propositions about Y → their supports
What does this verse mean verse → tokens/lemmas → interpretation questions → readings by lens → story context
Who is "he" here verse → discourse state → cast entry (story layer), answered without search
Word study lemma → occurrences (code) → sense propositions → usage in period writings
How has this been read over time verse → assertions ordered by date → dependsOn → first attestation
Compare A and B two lenses → aligned propositions → agree / differ / qualify
Is it true / historically credible proposition → supports and attacks across lenses → defeat conditions
Factual (who, when, how many) KB / chronology query + calculate
List (duties, marks, means) propositions about Y with role "item" across catechisms → union, each with its proof text

A plan is a template plus parameters. The model proposes a modification only when no template fits, and code checks the modification's types, dependencies and budget. Independent operations run in parallel. Each run is recorded as an EP-Plan trace.

Claim Must rest on Code checks

Taken together at least 2 map nodes and a stated connection labelled as synthesis
Totals, dates, counts cited values recomputed
Nothing earlier is known a recorded graph query and its scope the query is in the trace

Because claims are rendered from map nodes, most checks run before drafting, on the map, instead of after, on the prose. Today 44% of answers go through a revision costing about 23 s; that should shrink to the residual cases. Code cannot decide whether an interpretation is right. It decides whether the claim matches what its source says, who said it, and with what standing.

9. Skills, models and learning

  • Skills become plan-and-render specifications (ase:SkillPackage). Each names its competency template, its lens rules, the argument map's required slots, the output structure (a direct-answer field first), examples including failures, and tests. Skills are improved offline from traces (GEPA), with the evidence and permission rules frozen.
  • Our own models do the bulk, bounded jobs on RunPod: the propositioniser, the attributor (speaker and stance), the cross-lingual aligner, the edge scorer and the reranker over propositions. Each has its own held-out test against Haiku-labelled, reviewer-checked samples. Haiku stays the composer and labels the training samples.
  • The graph learns from questions. A slot the graph could not fill (§6.5) is logged with its anchors, and the most frequent gaps choose what the next extraction wave reads.

10. Evaluation

  • Two tracks: source-grounded, where the named source is allowed, and independent synthesis, where the reference source's graph is not loaded.
  • A proposition-level score. The reference answers are propositionised the same way, and coverage is measured as matched propositions. This is closer to "claims covered" than a holistic judgement, and it can be audited.
  • Graph quality is measured on its own: attribution and stance precision on a reviewed sample (target ≥ 0.9 for stance), anchor accuracy and alignment precision.
  • Ablations: graph vs passages, with and without the original-language spine, with and without lenses. Each runs on the same question ids, with three repeats, compared pairwise.
  • The judge is calibrated against blinded human review before any number is quoted.

11. The build, the first experiment and the kill criteria

11.1 The first experiment: one locus, end to end

Pick one doctrinal area with many dev-slice questions and rich sources: justification and faith (Romans, Galatians, James 2; the confessions; Augustine, Chrysostom, Luther, Calvin, Trent).

  1. Project what exists for those anchors: doctrine records, cross-references, interpretation claims.
  2. Extract propositions from the wave-1 sources on those books only (a few thousand units), labelled by Haiku and admitted by code.
  3. Anchor the Greek of Romans 3–5, Galatians 2–3 and James 2 to the Fathers' Greek commentaries on them.
  4. Build path search, the lenses and the argument map, keeping the Haiku composer fixed.
  5. Run the dev questions on that locus plus 30 new, independently written ones, on both tracks, compared pairwise against today's harness.

11.2 Kill criteria (decided before the run)

  • Continue if claims-covered rises by at least 0.15 on the independent track, restraint and Scripture support do not drop, and median time falls.
  • Stop and rethink if attribution or stance precision on the reviewed sample is below 0.85. A graph that misattributes is worse than passages.
  • Keep only the parts that earn it. If path search adds nothing over proposition retrieval alone, ship proposition retrieval (Dense X) without the beam.

11.3 Then

Phase Scope
P1 The locus experiment above, with the two-track reviewed slice
P2 Wave 1 across all loci; our propositioniser and attributor replace Haiku labelling
P3 The original-language spine across the Greek and Hebrew writings; lemma retrieval
P4 All question families on competency templates; community summaries; streaming
P5 Waves 2–3 by demand; offline skill optimisation; the 477-question suite against Apologist and Bonfire

Release gate (independent track, same question ids, calibrated judge): addresses the question ≥ 0.88, covers the reference ≥ 0.65, restraint ≥ 0.92, Scripture support ≥ 0.90, strict good ≥ 0.40. Each must be clearly above both reference apps.

12. What stays from today

  • The signed corpus, commitments and citation verification; the evidence rules; the separation of layers (Scripture · period · later).
  • The writings index and passage retrieval, as the fallback for slots the graph cannot fill.
  • The blind judge, frozen worktree snapshots per tag, the dev slice and the 477-question suite.
  • Haiku 5.5 as the composer.

13. Risks

  • Misattribution at scale. Stance errors (a view reported vs a view held) are the dangerous ones. They are measured first, and they gate the build.
  • The graph as hidden authority. A proposition is never cited as what Scripture says. Answers cite spans, and review status is visible.
  • Lens bias. The consensus lens depends on which traditions the library holds. Holdings per tradition are reported, and "broadly held" is said only when the graph shows it.
  • Size. 3.87M units may yield 20–40M propositions. Reading in waves, by demand, keeps that bounded, and the projection lives in its own database.
  • Over-engineering. The kill criteria exist so that the beam search, the lenses or the spine are dropped if they do not pay for themselves.

14. Precedents

These are research results on other corpora, not predicted gains here.

  • Dense X Retrieval (Chen et al., EMNLP 2024): propositions (atomic, self-contained factoids) used as the retrieval unit outperform passages and sentences, both in retrieval and in downstream QA under a fixed token budget. A small trained "propositionizer" produces them.
  • PropRAG (Wang & Han, EMNLP 2025): context-rich propositions are extracted offline into a graph, and an online beam search over proposition paths, with no model calls, answers multi-step questions. The authors argue that triples lose context that propositions keep.
  • HippoRAG 2: personalised PageRank over a knowledge graph integrated with passages.
  • GraphRAG: community summaries over an entity graph, for broad, "global" questions.
  • Decontextualisation (Choi et al., 2021): rewriting a sentence so that it stands alone.
  • Argument Interchange Format and Walton's argumentation schemes; micropublications and nanopublications: claims with their evidence and attribution, stored as graph records.
  • Competency questions (ontology engineering): the questions an ontology must answer, serving as both its requirements and its test.
  • LLMCompiler, doc2query, contextual retrieval and GEPA, as in revision 2.
