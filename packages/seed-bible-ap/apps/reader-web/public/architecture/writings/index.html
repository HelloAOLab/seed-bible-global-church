Period writings: publishing, indexing and translating them

Next to a verse, the reader can show what else was written about it: 1 Enoch beside Genesis 6, Josephus beside the Flood, Rashi beside every verse of the Torah, Chrysostom preaching on the same chapter. None of this comes from a model's memory. Every writing is an open-licence edition, published as a signed work, indexed by verse, name, source word and meaning, and described in an ontology that records what the work is, which edition we hold, its date and what we are allowed to do with it. Where a writing has no free English, the programme drafts its own English from the original language, labels it as a draft, and publishes it through the same path. This page covers all three.

What we may hold, and what that decides

Rights are recorded per edition, never per author: a Greek text of Josephus and Whiston's English are two editions with two licences. The licence decides three things: whether a source is published as a work at all, which partition it is indexed in, and whether an answer may quote it.

Licence of the edition Published as a work? Indexed? Quoted in an answer?
Public domain · CC0 · CC BY Yes, free lane Yes Yes, with attribution
CC BY-SA (Wikisource transcriptions) Yes, flagged share-alike Yes; derived records stay share-alike Yes, with attribution
CC BY-NC (Open Dead Sea, ETCSL) Yes, non-commercial lane only Yes, in its own partition Yes; never in a paid lane, never a translation input
By permission (a study Bible's notes) Yes, terms on the edition record Yes, in a partition never exported As the permission allows
Unknown or all rights reserved No. A work record only Title and date only Cited, never quoted

That last row is the reason the translation programme exists. Most of what Charlesworth, Pritchard's ANET, Soncino and the modern critical editions contain cannot be held. Where the original-language text is in the public domain, we can hold the original and make our own English from it.

Publishing: one work per edition, one block per unit

  1. Snapshot. One importer per edition fetches the source once and pins its bytes, with their hash, under ~/eval-store/ (never in a repository). The licence is stored with its evidence URL. A rerun reads the snapshot, not the web, so a source that changes upstream never changes a published work silently.
  2. Cut into citable units. The unit is the edition's own smallest citable piece: a verse of 1 Enoch (Charles numbers them, including his transposed 6a./7c.), a section of Josephus, one comment of Rashi on one verse, a paragraph of Augustine, a scroll line. Free-standing editor's notes are their own units. A note marker the source lost is never re-attached by guess.
  3. Place on a verse by citation only. A unit is linked to a verse only when its own text or its editor's notes cite the verse ("Gen. vi. 4", "Genesis 6:4", "Jude 6, 7"), when the publisher's own structure says so (Sefaria's reference for a Rashi comment), or when the owner's research atlas names the link. Nothing is placed by similarity. Hebrew-numbered sources are converted to the English numbering through the pinned versification file, and a verse outside the canon is a refusal at publish time, never a silent miss. The method of every link travels with it: text citation, note citation, publisher structure, atlas.
  4. Write the edition record. One record per edition: title, editor or translator, the publication, the licence and its evidence, the snapshot pin and hash, the work's date with its basis (atlas, chronology, or the edition's own statement; undated if none of them dates it), its group, and the status of the English.
  5. Publish. The edition becomes a writing-text work on Source Publishing, under the publisher identity @demo-admin-1: a README part (the front matter above) and one part per book or volume, at most 1.5 MB each. Each block is headed by its label, which carries the edition's own locator and, where placed, the verses it bears on: 
    [[Rashi on Genesis 6:4 | Gen.6.4]]
[[1 Enoch 6:2 | Gen.6.2]]
[[City of God XV.23 ¶4 | Gen.6.1-4]]
[[Against Celsus IV.ii]]            ← no verse: found by its own locator
    The gateway parses every label as it publishes and writes the verse index and the locator index. It refuses a label it cannot read.
  6. Read it back, then write the receipt. A success response is not proof. Every part is read back from the gateway and its commitment is recomputed. Only then does the work enter the receipt (published.json), which is what the indexer reads. A partial edition that a complete one replaces is withdrawn (a tombstone), never deleted.

Today: 150 works from 89 editions are live and in the receipt. They include the Pseudepigrapha (Charles), Josephus (Whiston), the deuterocanon (Brenton), the Testaments of the Twelve, the Apostolic Fathers, the Sefaria commentators and Targums across the Hebrew Bible (one edition per commentator, language and licence), the Ante-Nicene and Nicene Fathers by volume, and the programme's own drafts. The Dead Sea Scrolls (1,027 manuscripts) are published the same way, as 28 works in the non-commercial lane.

Indexing

The indexer reads the published works back and writes six projections: by verse (with the method of each link), by name (persons, places and things the knowledge base holds, with ambiguity kept, so four Johns stay four), by source word (a Strong's number and its Septuagint equivalent, so a word reaches its verses with no English in between), by meaning (a multilingual embedding, bge-m3, in Vectorize, with one fixed score floor), the chapter record and the book windows. It is the only writer, and every unit carries its work's date with the basis for it, its licence, its partition and a link to its signed block. About 242,000 units are indexed today. The full table, with sizes, is on the Scripture harness page.

One rule is decided by code at query time: layer. Each book of the Bible has a date window (its composition end, else its earliest witness, else its earliest dated copy). A writing dated inside the window is the passage's own period. A writing dated after it is a later reading and is shown as reception. A writing with no date is undated, and is never placed. Nothing in the later layer is ever given to the Bible translation's drafter.

The ontology: textual sources and writings

The writings are described by two bounded contexts, authored as Turtle in the skills repository and mirrored in GraphDB. Until 27 October 2026 one domain, scripture-writings, held both the Bible's own copies and every other text; it was split that day. Textual sources (txt:, https://skills.demo/textual-sources#) is the upper domain: the classes below, which any text shares. Writings (wrt:, https://skills.demo/writings#) holds everything that is not the Bible — its records in the A-box namespace wrtx: and the scroll compositions in wrtd:. The Bible's own witnesses stay in the Scripture domain: the canon's A-box is scrc:, the biblical scroll lines scrd:. All of it inherits from the agentic-trust upper ontology, which grounds on DOLCE, PROV-O and the Web Annotation model. The upper domain separates five things that are easy to merge by mistake:

Class What it is Grounded on Example
txt:SourceWork The work as composed: author, language, group, composition date range with its argument, attribution certainty, the register entry and the chronology key. dul:InformationObject, prov:Entity 1 Enoch; Seder Olam Rabbah
txt:TextEdition One edition of a work: editor, translator, language, where it was published, the licence and its evidence, the allowed operations, the export partition, when and from where it was acquired, the snapshot hash, and its published form (a wrt:PublishedWriting: the work's address, version and parts on Source Publishing, recorded only after every part was read back). at:InformationArtifact Whiston's Antiquities (1737, public domain)
txt:TextualWitness A physical carrier of a text: date, where it is held, its preservation. A witness date is never guessed. at:InformationArtifact the Great Isaiah Scroll, 1QIsaᵃ
txt:SourceSpan A located piece of an edition or a witness. It stores text only where the edition allows store; otherwise it says textUnavailable. oa:SpecificResource, prov:Entity 1QIsaᵃ 33:2
txt:PassageAlignment The claim that a span bears on a passage: the relation (cites · quotes · comments-on · retells · parallels · witness), the method, and the status (proposed · reviewed · quarantined). at:Observation Jubilees 5 retells Gen 6, by Charles's margin
txt:CanonClassification Which community counts a work as canonical, and since when. It is a classification of the work, never a property of it. dul:Classification 1 Enoch: canonical in the Ethiopian Orthodox Church

The rights vocabularies (txt:AllowedOperation: read · quote · store · export-by · export-sa · reference-only; txt:ExportPartition: by · sa · licensed · restricted) are SKOS concept schemes in the upper domain. They are the same values the indexer filters on, so a paid lane can never retrieve a non-commercial text.

design rule GraphDB holds records, D1 holds blocks

The graph holds work, edition, witness and alignment records, never a triple per published block. A block-level A-box would be millions of triples that say nothing a D1 row does not already say. It would also feed the store's reasoner, which has already filled the disk once through a single symmetric and transitive DOLCE relation. The Dead Sea Scrolls are the precedent: their own named graph holds one file per manuscript (the manuscript as a witness, its spans with textUnavailable, and its alignments) and no text.

Translating what has no free English

Much of what was written around Scripture in its own period, and much of what was written about it soon after, has never been translated into English, or only into an English still in copyright. The programme drafts its own English from a public-domain original-language edition, publishes it under CC BY 4.0 (CC BY-SA 4.0 where the transcription it was made from is share-alike), and places it beside the open-licence works through the same path. Before drafting, it checks what free English exists. Seder Olam Rabbah is the example: the Sefaria community translation (CC0) covers about a third of chapters 1–2, Feuer's (CC BY) chapter 3 only, nothing covers chapter 4, and Guggenheimer's 1998 English is in copyright.

Every drafted unit carries the original beside it and a status line that says exactly what was done: 

machine-aided draft from the Ancient Greek, claude-haiku-5-5: drafted, then checked
and revised against the original by the model; not yet reviewed by a person

A drafted edition has one of three statuses, and only a person can move it to the third:

Status Meaning
machine-draft one model pass, not checked
model-revised drafted, then checked clause by clause and revised against the original by the model; no person has reviewed it
human-reviewed a person reviewed it against the named edition

The translation approach, in detail

The aim is an English that a scholar can read next to the original and trust to say what the original says: faithful, plain, and with every place where the English had to decide something marked in a note. Five commitments shape every step.

  1. Translate the witness, not a better text. The English renders the text as this edition prints it. If a number or a name looks wrong, the printed reading stays in the English and the doubt goes into a note. (The first Seder Olam draft quietly changed an age from fourteen to three "as emended"; in a chronicle, that is fatal.)
  2. No English Bible in the room. When a writing quotes Scripture, the drafter translates the words in front of it and never substitutes the wording of an English Bible. Notes explain and never quote one.
  3. Code decides what code can decide. The model drafts, criticises and revises. Code checks coverage, script, length, emendation markers, quoted claims and kinship, and refuses a result that fails. A model is never the gate.
  4. Never silent, never changing. Every passage is cached by a hash of the model, the pipeline version and the original text. A rerun costs nothing and a published draft never changes without a new pipeline version.
  5. One pipeline, written as data. Every prompt, tool schema and code rule lives in one file, writings-pipeline.json (currently v2i-relation-triples). The importer and the translation runtime's writings steps both read it, so no prompt exists in two places. All model calls use Claude Haiku 5.5.

Getting the original into passages

Most originals reach us as scans of 19th-century printed editions, and OCR is the first source of error. The cutting is done per edition, by code, from marks that can be trusted:

  • Find reliable marks, not headings. In Migne's Chrysostom, the OCR misread the homily numbers (ΚΕ΄ came out as "ΟἌΠΛΙΑ Κ΄."), and the first cut put four homilies under Homily 22's title. A homily's first section mark, α΄. on a line of its own after the Scripture lemma, is reliable. The cut now runs from the blank line before each lemma to the next one.
  • Keep one language. Migne prints a Greek column and a facing Latin translation, and the OCR reads the Latin as Greek capitals. Only lines that read as Greek prose (mostly Greek letters, mostly lower case with accents) are kept. In Dindorf's Syncellus the Latin is kept, but only to resolve OCR damage, and the critical apparatus is skipped.
  • Strip what is not text. Margin line numbers (the Weimar Luther), running headers, page numbers and apparatus lines.
  • Cut at sentence ends. A passage is about 1,800–2,600 characters, closed at a full stop, colon, semicolon, Greek ano teleia or Hebrew sof pasuq, so no sentence is split across two passages. A work in chapters is drafted chapter by chapter, and each chapter becomes a part with its own locator.

The six steps for each passage

  1. Draft, with the tradition's terms in view. The drafter is given the passage, a short note on what the text is (for example "the numbers, the names and the scripture quotations carry the sense; keep the arithmetic exact"), and only those entries of the translation glossary whose source form occurs in the passage (for example יצר הרע → "the evil inclination", with the transliteration given once in a note). It is told the three faults a literal pass makes: flattening a term of art, rendering an idiom or euphemism word for word, and confusing look-alike roots. OCR damage it cannot resolve is marked [?] and an unreadable passage [illegible].
  2. A critic that walks every clause. A second call reads the original against the draft, in windows of about 400 characters, and accounts for every clause in order: the draft's English for it, the root of each content word and its alternatives, and any fault, classed as one of nine kinds: term-of-art, idiom, lexical, sense, omission, addition, OCR, name, scripture. Code checks that the clauses it returned cover the original, and asks again if they do not. (A critic asked for "faults" skimmed and missed three of the four the outside review found. One asked to account for every clause has to look at each.)
  3. Revise, with translator's notes. A third call fixes every fault it agrees with, keeps the rest of the draft as it is, and attaches a translator's note, anchored to the exact English words, wherever the English settles a euphemism, a disputed reading or a term of art. Code refuses a revision that is still in the source script or has lost a third of the draft's length, and keeps the draft.
  4. Restore the witness. Code looks for emendation markers in the English ("so the emended text", "read …"). Where it finds one, a call puts the printed reading back and moves the doubt into a note that says what the printed text has, why it is doubted, and what editors read.
  5. Hold every note to the source. A note may gloss a word, name a term, explain an idiom or state a textual doubt. It may not add a genealogical, historical or geographical claim the passage does not make. Each note is split into its factual claims, each pronoun is resolved to the person the English sentence means, and each claim must quote the exact words of the English or the original that state it. Code keeps a note only if every quoted claim is really there. (A note once said "Yoktan is Eber's brother"; the passage's "his brother" is Peleg's.)
  6. Decide kinship and ages by code. The notes check's answer to "do these words state the claim?" changed from one run to the next, so it no longer decides the claims a chronicle cannot get wrong. For a kinship claim in a note, one narrow call lists the relations (person, relation, of whom) stated by the single English sentence that holds the quoted words, with each pronoun resolved inside that sentence. A second call lists the relations in the claim the same way. Code keeps the claim only if its relations are among the sentence's. For an age or a span of years, months, days or generations, code alone holds every number in the claim to a number the English or the original carries. A bare number, such as a scripture citation, is not held this way.

Every step came from a found fault. v2 came from an outside review of the Yalkut draft (four kinds of fault a literal pass makes). v2b, the clause-by-clause critic, came from a critic that skimmed. v2d, the witness and notes steps, came from the Seder Olam draft's emended age and its wrong note. v2e, quoted claims, came from a notes check that "corrected" a wrong note into a worse one. The fix goes into the step that should have caught the fault, never into the draft by hand.

How it is measured

Two kinds of evaluation, kept apart. Both are judged by Claude Haiku 5.5 three times, and neither has yet been adjudicated by a person, so the numbers are diagnostics, not quality claims.

review points Does the draft say what a reviewer said it must?

Each point names the source words, what the English must do, and the kind of fault it tests. On Yalkut Shimoni §44, six points: the single literal pass (v1) met 3; every pipeline from v2c on meets 5. The moral comparison ("worse than human beings", not "harsher"), the idiom ("consent", not "listen to me") and the term of art are now right. The lexical point is still missed by every pipeline: שתזכו ("that you may merit") is still read as "remembered".

reference pairs Against a published English the pipeline never sees

For Hebrew works that do have a public-domain English, the pipeline drafts without ever showing that English to the drafter, critic or reviser. Two readings follow. The first is chrF, a character-overlap score, as a closeness diagnostic and never a target. The second is a judge that reads the source, our draft and the held English in random order and says which carries the source's sense, listing each divergence and which side is wrong. A divergence where the held English is wrong is counted, not penalised. A control (the same text on both sides) read "both" on 53 of 54 readings.

The judge is the same model family as the drafter, which is why these readings wait for a person's adjudication before anyone quotes them as quality. The workbench's Translations page shows each work's review points one by one and the pairs as cards.

The next sources, and the hard case

The pipeline does not change with the source. What changes is how the original is found, cut and dated. Three families come next.

ready Migne's Greek and Latin: cut and drafted like Chrysostom

The Patrologia Graeca and Patrologia Latina are public domain, and the scans are on the Internet Archive. Each work is cut the way Chrysostom was: locate it by marks the OCR reads reliably (section marks, the Scripture lemma, column numbers), not by headings it garbles; keep the Greek column and drop the facing Latin (or, for a Latin work, the Latin alone, with margin numbers stripped); close passages at sentence ends. Then it goes through the six steps unchanged. A Latin work goes through the same pipeline as Luther's Weimar Latin did. Each importer is a function returning the passages and the edition's metadata, so a new work is one function and its verification of the cut, nothing else.

ready Sefaria's rabbinic Hebrew: straight through the Seder Olam importer

Sefaria's text arrives already digitised and structured, with each version's licence stated. There is no OCR and no column to separate. The Seder Olam importer fetches a reference in a named version through the snapshotting client, flattens its sections, cuts at sentence ends, drafts chapter by chapter and assembles one edition whose parts are the chapters. Another work is a new entry in its table: the reference, the version name, the date with its basis, and a one-line note on what kind of text it is. Two checks come first: a public-domain or open-licence Hebrew version must exist, and the free English must be missing or incomplete. Where a free English exists, the work goes to the reference-pairs track instead, and becomes a measurement.

hard case Ancient Near Eastern texts: a draft carries a dated text

For Akkadian, Sumerian and Ugaritic texts, the editions we may hold are old: transliterations and copies published a century or more ago, now in the public domain. The current corpora either restrict their use or are in copyright, and since those old editions appeared, the readings have been corrected. Signs have been re-read, fragments joined, lines restored differently. A faithful draft from an old transliteration is therefore a faithful translation of that editor's reading, as of that year, and not of the text as it is read today. The pipeline's first rule, to translate the witness as printed, makes that exact. It also makes it dangerous if the date is not said. So for this family:

  • The edition's reading is the witness. The draft renders the transliteration as printed, including the editor's restorations, which stay marked as restorations. It never "corrects" toward a reading the model remembers from later scholarship. Without an edition, that would be an unattributed emendation.
  • The date is on every unit. Each status line says whose reading, and when ("from the transliteration of [editor], [year]"), beside the work's own date. The edition record carries the reading's date as a field of its own, so the reader and the Ask harness can say "a 1902 reading" rather than present it as the text.
  • Later corrections are referenced, not merged. Where a current edition we may only cite reads a line differently, the unit can carry a note naming that edition and its line, never its wording. Where the newer reading is under a non-commercial licence that we can hold, it is published as its own edition in the non-commercial lane, and the two are aligned to each other. One is never silently replaced by the other.
  • It stays out of the Bible translation. Like every writing, it is evidence for readers and for the Ask harness. It is never an input to the drafter of the Bible.

This family is designed, not yet built. The first work will show whether a status line and a note are enough, or whether a dated reading needs its own status value beside machine-draft, model-revised and human-reviewed.
